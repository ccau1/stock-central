# Why You Must Invest

Saving money is not enough. If your savings sit in cash, they are quietly losing purchasing power every year. Investing is not just a way to grow wealth — it is a defense against a monetary system designed to dilute cash.

## The Cost of Holding Cash

Since 1971, the U.S. dollar has lost the vast majority of its purchasing power. What $1 could buy in 1971 now requires about $8 today.

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Cumulative inflation (1971–2024) | ~677% |
| Average annual inflation | ~3.94% |
| Purchasing power of $1 in 1971 | ~$0.13 today |

In other words, a dollar saved in 1971 and left under a mattress would buy roughly one-eighth of what it used to. A bank account paying 0.5% interest does only slightly better.

> Inflation is when you pay fifteen dollars for the ten-dollar haircut you used to get for five dollars when you had hair.
> — Sam Ewing

## What Is Fiat Currency?

**Fiat currency** is money that has value because a government declares it to be legal tender. It is not backed by a physical commodity like gold or silver. Its value comes from trust in the issuing government and the demand for that currency in trade.

Most modern currencies — the U.S. dollar, the euro, the yen, the pound — are fiat. This system gives governments enormous flexibility, but it also removes the hard constraint that a commodity-backed system imposes on money creation.

## The Nixon Shock: How the Rules Changed

In 1944, the Bretton Woods system made the U.S. dollar the world’s reserve currency, pegged to gold at $35 per ounce. Other currencies were pegged to the dollar. For decades, foreign governments could redeem U.S. dollars for gold.

On August 15, 1971, President Richard Nixon announced that the United States would temporarily suspend the convertibility of dollars into gold. The move was framed as temporary. It became permanent.

### Consequences of Ending the Gold Standard

- The dollar became a pure fiat currency.
- The Bretton Woods system collapsed, leading to floating exchange rates.
- Governments were no longer constrained by gold reserves when creating money.
- The door opened to the large-scale monetary expansion we see today.

## Quantitative Easing: Money Creation at Scale

**Quantitative easing (QE)** is a form of unconventional monetary policy in which a central bank creates new money electronically and uses it to buy government bonds and other securities. The stated goal is to lower interest rates, encourage lending, and stimulate the economy.

### How QE Expanded the Fed’s Balance Sheet

| Period | Approximate Fed Balance Sheet |
|--------|-------------------------------|
| Pre-2008 | Under $1 trillion |
| After QE1–QE3 (2014) | ~$4.5 trillion |
| Peak after 2020 QE | ~$8.9 trillion |

The Fed’s balance sheet grew roughly tenfold between 2005 and the early 2020s. Much of this expansion came from electronically created reserves used to purchase financial assets.

## QE and Inequality

QE does not distribute money equally. Because it works largely through financial markets, its first and largest effects show up in asset prices — stocks, bonds, and real estate.

| Group | Typical Exposure to QE-Driven Assets |
|-------|--------------------------------------|
| Wealthy households | High ownership of stocks, real estate, private assets |
| Middle class | Some exposure through homes and retirement accounts |
| Lower-income households | Mostly cash, wages, and consumer debt |

When the central bank buys bonds and pushes interest rates down, borrowers benefit and asset owners benefit. But households that rely on wages and savings accounts see little direct gain, while their cost of living gradually rises.

Research and policy reviews — including work by the Bank of England and academic economists — have found that QE can widen wealth inequality because the wealthy own the assets that rise in price.

## Inflation: The Silent Tax

Inflation is often described as a general rise in prices. Another way to look at it: inflation is a decline in the purchasing power of money. For people holding cash or earning fixed wages, that decline acts like a tax.

### How Inflation Transfers Wealth

- **Governments and debtors** benefit because they repay debt with money that is worth less than when they borrowed it.
- **Savers and wage earners** lose because the same number of dollars buys fewer goods and services.
- **Asset owners** often preserve or grow purchasing power because stocks, real estate, and commodities tend to rise with or above inflation.

This is why cash is a poor long-term store of value. It does not earn enough to offset the steady loss of purchasing power.

## Why Investing Is the Defense

Investing is how you convert cash into productive or scarce assets that can outpace inflation over time.

| Asset Class | Long-Term Role |
|-------------|----------------|
| Stocks | Growth; historically outpaces inflation |
| Real estate | Income and inflation hedge |
| Commodities | Inflation hedge and diversification |
| Bonds | Stability and income |
| Cash | Liquidity only, not a store of value |

A diversified portfolio is not a guarantee, but it is the most practical way for ordinary people to protect and grow purchasing power in a fiat system.

## Key Takeaways

- Fiat money has no physical backing, so its supply can expand without a hard limit.
- The 1971 Nixon Shock removed the dollar’s gold peg and ushered in the modern fiat era.
- Quantitative easing has massively expanded central bank balance sheets and asset prices.
- Inflation quietly erodes the value of cash, which hurts savers and wage earners.
- The wealthy tend to benefit more from QE because they own the assets that rise in value.
- Investing is not optional for long-term wealth preservation — it is a necessary defense.
