# Historical Investment Growth

Long-term investing works because markets have historically rewarded patient capital. Below are data-driven snapshots for two of the most common asset classes: U.S. large-cap stocks and U.S. residential real estate.

## S&P 500: Decades of Compound Growth

The S&P 500 tracks 500 of the largest publicly traded U.S. companies and is widely used as a proxy for the overall stock market.

### S&P 500 Key Numbers

| Period | Average Annual Return | Source |
|--------|----------------------|--------|
| 1957–2024 | ~10.5% | [Investopedia — S&P 500 historical performance](https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/042415/what-average-annual-return-sp-500.asp) |
| 1964–2024 | ~10.5% CAGR (compound), ~11.9% arithmetic average | [Slickcharts — annual total-return data](https://www.slickcharts.com/sp500/returns) |
| 1928–2024 | ~10.1% | [NYU Stern / Aswath Damodaran historical returns data](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/histretSP.html) |

### What This Means in Practice

A **10.5% annual return** turns $10,000 into about **$190,000** over 30 years, assuming dividends are reinvested and taxes are ignored.

The actual path is never smooth. The S&P 500 has experienced major drawdowns, including:

- **1973–1974**: ~-45% from peak to trough
- **2000–2002**: dot-com bust
- **2007–2009**: financial crisis, ~-57% drawdown
- **2022**: -18% total return

Despite these crises, the index has historically recovered and reached new highs over multi-year periods. This is why long time horizons and consistent contributions matter more than perfect timing.

## U.S. Real Estate: Steadier but Slower Growth

Residential real estate has historically grown more slowly than stocks, but it has also been less volatile. Returns come from two sources: price appreciation and rental income (or implied rental value for owner-occupied homes).

### Real Estate Key Numbers

| Period | Average Annual Return / Appreciation | Source |
|--------|--------------------------------------|--------|
| 1992–2024 | ~5.5% (price appreciation, Case-Shiller) | [Investopedia — Case-Shiller analysis](https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/052015/which-has-performed-better-historically-stock-market-or-real-estate.asp) |
| 1928–2023 | ~4.2% (housing returns) | [A Wealth of Common Sense — historical housing returns](https://awealthofcommonsense.com/2024/01/what-is-the-historical-rate-of-return-on-housing/) |
| 1890–2006 | ~11.3% gross total return (3.8% capital gain + 7.5% rental return) | [Philadelphia Fed working paper](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/frbp/assets/working-papers/2024/wp24-12.pdf) |

### Important Distinctions

- **Price-only indices** like the Case-Shiller index measure home-price appreciation, not total return.
- **Total return** for a rental property includes rental income, which has historically made up a large portion of housing returns.
- **Your primary residence** is partly a lifestyle expense and partly an investment. Its return depends heavily on location, leverage, maintenance costs, and how long you stay.

## Comparing the Two

| Factor | S&P 500 | U.S. Residential Real Estate |
|--------|---------|------------------------------|
| Long-term nominal return | ~10% | ~4–5.5% (price); higher with rental income |
| Volatility | Higher | Lower |
| Liquidity | High (sell in seconds) | Low (selling a home takes weeks or months) |
| Diversification | Owns 500 companies | Concentrated in one location |
| Ongoing costs | Fund fees / trading costs | Maintenance, property tax, insurance |

## The Bottom Line

History suggests that a diversified stock portfolio has delivered stronger long-term growth than residential real estate, while real estate has offered more stability and income. Most long-term investors benefit from owning **both**, in proportions that match their goals, time horizon, and ability to handle volatility.

> **Remember:** past performance does not guarantee future results. These figures are useful for setting expectations, not for predicting next year's returns.
