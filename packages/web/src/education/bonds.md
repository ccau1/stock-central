# Bonds and the Economic Cycle

Bonds are often described as the "safe" part of a portfolio, but they are not risk-free. Their role changes depending on where the economy is in its cycle, and today they carry a new risk: the financial health of the U.S. government itself.

## What Are Bonds?

A bond is a loan. When you buy a government or corporate bond, you are lending money to the issuer in exchange for regular interest payments and the return of your principal at maturity.

| Feature | What It Means |
|---------|---------------|
| Principal | The original amount lent |
| Coupon | The interest payments received |
| Maturity | When the principal is repaid |
| Yield | The effective return based on price and coupon |
| Duration | Sensitivity to interest-rate changes |

Bonds are generally less volatile than stocks and can provide steady income. That makes them attractive during uncertain times — but only when the borrower is trustworthy.

## Stocks vs. Bonds Through the Cycle

Stocks and bonds often move in opposite directions depending on the economic cycle.

| Cycle Phase | Typical Stock Behavior | Typical Bond Behavior |
|-------------|------------------------|-----------------------|
| Recovery / early expansion | Strong performance | Modest or weak performance |
| Late expansion | Moderate, then slowing | Under pressure as rates rise |
| Contraction / recession | Sharp declines | Often rally as rates fall and investors seek safety |
| Early recovery | Begin to recover | Continue to perform as rates stay low |

This is why a classic balanced portfolio holds both. When stocks fall, high-quality bonds can act as a cushion. When the economy is strong, stocks drive growth.

### The 60/40 Portfolio

A traditional balanced portfolio is 60% stocks and 40% bonds. The idea is to capture most of the stock market's upside while using bonds to reduce volatility and provide income.

However, this relationship breaks down when both stocks and bonds fall at the same time — something that happened in 2022 when rising inflation and interest rates hurt both asset classes.

## Why Bonds Matter in a Portfolio

Even with their limitations, bonds serve important roles:

- **Income** — regular interest payments
- **Stability** — lower volatility than stocks
- **Diversification** — often zig when stocks zag
- **Capital preservation** — useful as a goal gets closer

For investors nearing retirement, bonds help protect the wealth they have already built. For younger investors, a smaller bond allocation usually makes sense because they have time to recover from stock market downturns.

## The Current Debt Problem

U.S. government bonds are still considered among the safest assets in the world. But the math behind U.S. debt has become harder to ignore.

### Key Numbers

| Metric | Figure |
|--------|--------|
| U.S. federal debt-to-GDP (FY2025) | ~124% |
| Annual federal deficit (FY2024–2025) | ~$1.8 trillion |
| Projected deficit by 2035 | ~$2.6 trillion |
| Net interest on debt (2025) | Nearly $1 trillion |

In simple terms, the U.S. government is spending far more than it collects in taxes. The gap is filled by issuing Treasury bonds. The more debt outstanding, the larger the interest bill becomes — and the more new debt must be issued just to pay old interest.

## Credit Rating Downgrades

Credit rating agencies assess how likely a borrower is to repay its debt. For decades, the United States held the highest possible rating from all three major agencies. That has changed.

| Agency | Previous Rating | Current Rating | Date of Change |
|--------|-----------------|----------------|----------------|
| S&P | AAA | AA+ | August 2011 |
| Fitch | AAA | AA+ | August 2023 |
| Moody's | Aaa | Aa1 | May 2025 |

For the first time in modern history, none of the three major rating agencies gives U.S. debt its top-tier rating. The reasons cited include:

- Persistent large fiscal deficits
- Rising debt-to-GDP ratios
- Growing interest costs
- Political gridlock and repeated debt-ceiling standoffs

## What This Means for Bond Investors

A downgrade does not mean the U.S. will default. The dollar remains the world's reserve currency and Treasuries are still deeply liquid. But the downgrades signal that the risk profile has shifted.

### Potential Consequences

- **Higher borrowing costs** — as ratings fall, lenders may demand higher yields.
- **More volatile bond prices** — long-term Treasuries could become more sensitive to fiscal news.
- **Reduced "safe haven" status** — in a crisis, investors may look elsewhere.
- **Greater need for diversification** — relying only on U.S. bonds may no longer be enough.

### Practical Takeaways

- Do not abandon bonds, but understand their risks.
- Consider shorter-duration bonds, which are less sensitive to rate changes.
- Diversify across geographies and bond types: Treasury, corporate, municipal, international.
- Pay attention to real yields — nominal yield minus inflation — to see what you are actually earning.

## The Bigger Picture

Bonds remain a useful tool, but the old rule that "U.S. Treasuries are risk-free" is increasingly questioned. Fiscal deficits, political dysfunction, and rising interest costs have changed the landscape. A thoughtful investor today treats bonds as one part of a diversified portfolio — not a guaranteed safe harbor.
